---

Biological and artificial neural networks differ substantially in their physical implementation, learning mechanisms, and dynamics. Biological Neural Information processing is recurrent, stochastic, embodied systems composed of heterogeneous cells that operate across multiple spatial and temporal scales. Artificial neural networks are usually trained with gradient-based optimisation and are often much simpler in both architecture and dynamics. Yet artificial neural networks can develop representations that predict neural responses or resemble the geometry of neural population activity. I find this convergence weirdly interesting. It raises an important question for me: when does representation similarity reflect a shared computational solution?

---

**Biological and Artificial Representations:** I am interested in moving beyond the question of whether a model predict the bold activity. Instead, I want to ask which properties of a model, its architecture, objective, learning rule, dynamics, or inductive biases, make it align with the brain, and whether these properties can generate testable hypotheses about biological computation. One direction I am exploring is whether graph-based methods can reveal structure in the relationship between brain and model representations that conventional similarity metrics may miss. This includes asking how task structure, model architecture, and learning objectives shape the local and global geometry of representational spaces. In this context, I also find concepts of symmetry, invariance, and equivariance are particularly interesting.

**Neural dynamics and temporal computation:** Biological computation is fundamentally non-static: recurrent interactions, oscillations, behavioral states, and temporal context constantly shape what a neural population represents and how it processes information. However, attempting to capture these temporal dynamics in artificial systems reveals a critical observation. Models can often forecast a dynamical system with high accuracy while developing completely different internal geometries. If two models achieve the same behavioral output but represent the temporal data in entirely different ways, the performance metric becomes decoupled from the mechanistic reality. This raises a difficult but exciting question for me: what does a model actually learn about a dynamical system when task performance alone is not enough to distinguish between its internal representations? Could we use mechanistic interpretability techniques to unpack these task-relevant representations, and from there, infer the actual computational principles driving biological neural circuits?
